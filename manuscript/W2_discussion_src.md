## 8.1 The Mamdani layer in relation to prior work

The classical analysis of monotone Mamdani–Assilian models [[vbdb2008, vbdb2009]] asks which rule bases and output partitions produce monotone outputs, and its answer depends on the t-norm, the number of inputs and the smoothness of the rule base. Our analysis changes one design choice — how rules that share a consequent are combined — and in exchange obtains a statement that is uniform in the number of inputs and in the rule base. The mechanism is visible in Lemma 1: with ordered Ruspini partitions and product conjunction, the firing strengths form a product probability distribution, the bounded sum turns the consequent strengths into the push-forward of that distribution through the rule map, and a monotone rule map pushes stochastically larger inputs to stochastically larger labels. Nothing in this argument uses smoothness, which is why the non-smooth rule base of Table 3 stays monotone under the bounded sum and violates monotonicity under the maximum.

The bounded sum is not an exotic operator. With Ruspini partitions and product conjunction the partial sums never exceed one, so the bounded sum coincides with the ordinary sum used by additive fuzzy systems [[kosko1997]] and by sum–product systems [[kouikoglou2009]]. What differs from those models is the output side: we keep clipping implication and the maximum across different consequents, a common default configuration of fuzzy-logic toolboxes. For the fully additive model the defuzzifier condition reduces to Proposition 1, which follows from the centroid formula of the standard additive model; for the clipped model it does not have a closed form, and Proposition 2 replaces the missing formula with an exact finite check. The skewed consequents of Fig. 3b show that the check is not a formality: an otherwise perfectly built system can still be non-monotone when the output sets are chosen badly.

Table 3 also clarifies a practical point. When the product serves for both conjunction and implication, maximum aggregation produced no violation for our smooth rule base, in line with the positive two-input result for the product t-norm in [[vbdb2009]]; our particular non-smooth rule base happened to remain monotone as well. The violations arise from the *combination* of product (or minimum) conjunction with clipping implication and maximum aggregation — a common toolbox default, and the configuration of the original design of our screening model. Designers who prefer clipping, for instance because clipped consequents are easier to explain to clinicians, can keep it and restore monotonicity by changing only the aggregation of rules that share a consequent.

## 8.2 The Choquet layer in relation to prior work

Choquistic regression [[tehrani2011, tehrani2012]], later extended to ordinal outcomes [[tehrani2013]], introduced the Choquet integral into probabilistic classification with a guarantee of monotonicity, and it remains the reference point. Two later lines of work reduced its cost: a formulation specific to 2-additive capacities [[hullermeier2013]] and neural parametrizations that enforce nonnegativity of the weights of (5) by a softplus map [[bresson2020]] or represent the integral as a network [[islam2020]]. Our contribution is narrower and, we think, complementary: Theorem 2 and Propositions 3–4 state exactly when the 2-additive problem is convex, prove that the reparametrized penalized problem has the same solutions as the original one, and identify its unpenalized version with choquistic regression up to a change of variables. The practical consequence is that a standard bound-constrained quasi-Newton method finds the global optimum, with no linear constraints, no equality constraint for normalization and no multiple random starts. The normalized parametrization is still useful for interpretation; Proposition 4 shows how to recover it from the fitted model, and the Shapley values and interaction indices follow by dividing by C_m(1, …, 1).

A second difference concerns the penalty. The L1 penalty of choquistic regression acts on the normalized Möbius coefficients, which, as noted after Proposition 4, only shrinks negative interactions in the 2-additive case. Our penalty acts on the unnormalized interactions of both signs, and the complementarity of Proposition 3(b) makes it an exact L1 penalty on the interaction graph. Which of the two is preferable is an empirical question that we did not settle; our comparison with choquistic regression in Table 4 used its unpenalized maximum-likelihood version, which isolates the optimization problem but may understate the accuracy of a tuned penalized choquistic model.

## 8.3 Limitations

Several limitations qualify the results. First, the certificate of Proposition 2 is exact for a finite output grid, and Algorithm 1 is written for three output labels; for M > 3 the arrangement lives in a higher-dimensional simplex and the vertex enumeration grows accordingly, although it remains finite. Second, Lemma 1 relies on the product conjunction; with the minimum the firing strengths no longer sum to one, and although our system remained monotone in that configuration (Section 7.1), Theorem 1 does not cover it. Third, in the Choquet layer the direction of each raw input was estimated from the training fold; monotonicity is then guaranteed with respect to the oriented inputs, and an attribute whose relation to the outcome is not monotone can cost accuracy: on the Parkinson's data the unconstrained model had a higher mean AUC, although the difference was not significant, whereas on the Cleveland data, which contain nominal attributes, the constraints did no measurable harm. When the layer is fed by domain risks from the first layer, the direction is known by construction and this issue disappears. Fourth, we restricted the fusion layer to 2-additive capacities; higher-order interactions would need a different reparametrization. Fifth, the predictive evaluation of the Mamdani layer itself was not possible on POL252, which contains no age information; the layer was evaluated as a system, through its output surface, and not through its effect on discrimination. Finally, the data sets are small, the cost measurements were made on a shared workstation, and timings should be read as orders of magnitude rather than as benchmarks.
